Teaching in a room changes the way you write a question.

When thirty students make twenty different mistakes on the same problem, the answer key is not enough.

My physical classes typically include around 30–40 students. Remote teaching is intentionally smaller, usually one-to-one or groups of four to eight. Across both settings, I ask students to explain their approach before I explain mine.

PRACTICE PHILOSOPHY

Difficulty should come from reasoning, not unnecessary complication.

I deliberately use some practice that is slightly harder than the actual exam.

The goal is not ugly arithmetic or longer expressions. A useful hard question connects familiar SAT concepts in a way the student has not seen packaged before. Once that level feels manageable, ordinary exam questions feel less threatening.

THE LAST 50 POINTS

At the top end, mathematics is often no longer the main problem.

Careless signs, timing decisions, misreading the requested quantity and refusing to leave a question when the mind goes blank can matter more than another formula.

THE DISTRACTOR LAB

If a student chooses the mistake I expected, the option has already told me something.

I build wrong choices around realistic errors: sign mistakes, incomplete final steps, wrong quantities, bad formulas and plausible misinterpretations.
